How to read the 3D models

On the consonance charts page, a model in the corner of the screen follows the chart you are reading. This page explains its three views: the coin, the helix and the Cayley graph. They show the same facts as the charts, drawn as motions, so you can see why a chart looks the way it does. Intervals are zero-indexed, as on the charts: unison 0, 3rd 2, octave 7, 10th 9, 12th 11.

The idea in one paragraph

Fold every interval into one octave and there are seven of them: unison, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th. Put them round a seven-sided figure. A shift Jv then moves every interval at once, the same way, like turning a rigid shape. When the voices stay in order (Jv ≥ 0, or the larger intervals of an inverse shift) the shape turns. When the voices cross, each interval is read the other way up (§90), and the shape flips over, like a coin. Turns and flips are the only two motions there are, and the charts are a record of where each interval lands.

The coin: one octave

The two stacked heptagons are the top and bottom faces of one thin seven-sided coin. What you see:

  • The ring outside the coin stays still. Its seven slots are the intervals you hear after the shift, named on the outside. A filled dot is a consonance (unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th); a hollow one is a dissonance. Beside each slot are that interval’s own suspension marks, the derivative’s marks in the chart.
  • The numbered discs ride on the coin. Each is an interval m of the original, colored as in the chart’s bottom row (green fixed consonance, and so on). It carries its own marks, the upper voice’s mark above and the lower voice’s below.
  • Press Play again (Replay on the corner model) and each disc moves to the slot it becomes. A dashed line is the axis of a flip.

Try it: pick a Jv, drag to turn the model, and press Play again.

Reading a rule off the coin

Take Jv = −11 and follow disc 5, the 6th. All seven discs cross here, so the whole coin flips over the axis through the 3rd (n becomes 11 − n). Disc 5 lands on the slot of the 7th, a hollow dot: the 6th becomes a 7th, a variable consonance, so the disc is colored that way.

Before the flip the 6th shows --- above (upper voice) and x below (lower voice). After the flip it is upside down: the lower voice’s x is now on top and the upper voice’s --- underneath. That is §90 in one picture: in the inversion the voices have swapped.

Now compare the disc with its slot, side by side. On top, the lower voice’s x meets the 7th’s — (must be a suspension): together they make — x, which the chart prints below the 6th because it is the lower voice’s rule. Underneath, the upper voice’s --- meets the 7th’s (—) (can’t be suspended), and the stricter (—) is the rule printed above the 6th (§128). Look at the 6th in the −11 chart: (—) above, — x below.

So the rule of thumb is: compare each mark on a disc with the slot’s mark on the same side, and the result belongs to the voice whose mark that was. After a turn nothing is upside down, so top goes with top as you would expect.

When only some discs flip

At Jv = −4 (inversion at the 5th) only intervals smaller than 4 cross. Discs 0 to 3 flip; discs 4, 5 and 6 keep the voices in order and turn. The coin itself stays still, and the discs move one by one. The same happens at −2. At −7, by contrast, every disc flips together, over the axis through the unison.

The axis of a flip always passes through the midpoint of the unison and the interval of inversion, counted within the octave: the unison for the octave, the 2nd for the 10th, the 3rd for the 12th.

The helix: every register

The coin forgets the octave, so it cannot tell −7 from −14, or a 3rd from a 10th. The helix keeps them. It is the line of all intervals wound into a spiral, one turn per octave, rising with the interval: the unison at the middle, the octave one turn above, and below the unison the intervals with the voices crossed (marked ↓). Every corner of the coin is one column of the helix, seen from above.

Replay runs in two steps. First every disc slides along the spiral by Jv, to n = m + Jv. Then any disc that ended below the unison folds up through the unison, a half-turn, to the interval you actually hear, |n|, turned upside down. That fold is the crossing of §90.

Compare −7 and −14 here. At −14 every disc slides twice as far down the spiral, so the ones that cross fold up from an octave lower. Up to disc 9 they still end on consonances and dissonances in the same places, which is why the two charts agree that far. They part at 10 and 11 (the 11th and 12th): at −7 those become a 4th and a 5th, at −14 a 5th and a 4th.

The Cayley graph: how the charts fit together

Counted within the octave there are exactly 14 shifts: seven turns and seven flips. The graph puts each on a dot. The top ring holds the turns (voices in order: 0, +1, …, +6), the bottom ring the flips (the inversions at the octave, 9th, 10th and so on). Charts an octave apart, such as −11 and −4, are the same motion and share a dot.

  • A solid line is one more step of shift. Walking round the top ring adds a step each time.
  • A dashed line is the octave flip, the inversion at the octave. It joins each turn to the flip below it.
  • The number on a dot is how many of the four consonances that shift keeps consonant: 4 for the 10th, 3 for the octave and 12th. That is one way to see why Taneyev practices those three first.
  • A filled dot holds a shift Taneyev works through; tap it to see links to those charts.

Tap one dot, then another, to see what the two shifts make one after the other. For example, invert at the octave and then at the 10th, and the result is a direct shift up a 3rd: two flips make a turn. A turn and a flip always make a flip.

Naming each shift: T and I, words, cycles

The shortest names are music theory’s, the same ones used for pitch classes but counted in scale steps (mod 7): Ta is the turn that sends n to n + a, and Ik the flip that sends n to k − n. They read straight off the charts: Jv = +a is Ta and Jv = −k is Ik, with a and k taken mod 7. So the 12th (−11) is I₄, the 10th (−9) is I₂ and the octave (−7) is I₀. The subscript of Ik is what each swapped pair adds up to: I₄ swaps 0 and 4, 1 and 3, 5 and 6 (5 + 6 = 11, which is 4 mod 7).

Group theory writes the same 14 motions with two moves: r, a turn by one step (Jv = +1), and s, the octave flip (Jv = −7, which sends n to −n). Ta is ra, and Ik is rks: flip at the octave, then turn by k. So the inversion at the 12th (−11, and 11 is 4 mod 7) is r⁴s.

The same motion can be written in cycle notation, which lists where each corner of the coin goes. (0 4) means the unison and the 5th trade places; a corner left out stays put. A turn is one long cycle through all seven corners. A flip is three swaps, and the corner it leaves out is the one its axis passes through. Every model shows all three names in its top-left corner, for example I₄ = r⁴s = (0 4)(1 3)(5 6) at −11.

Two things to keep in mind. You need the sign as well as the number mod 7: +4 is a turn (T₄) but −3 is a flip (I₃). And an inversion smaller than the octave (−1 to −6) is two pieces: the intervals below k cross and flip, the rest stay in order and turn, so the models list both. At −4, for example, 0–3 flip by I₄ and 4–6 turn by T₃.

While you practice

  • Open the consonance charts. The model in the corner replays each chart you pick. Hide it with the Hide button; the page remembers your choice.
  • Use the coin to see the shape of a chart at a glance: which discs land on hollow slots (they become dissonant), and whether the coin flips as a whole or disc by disc.
  • Switch to the helix when the register matters, as with −7 and −14.
  • Switch to the Cayley graph to see how a chart relates to the others, for instance which inversions combine into which.

The group-theory reading (turns and flips, the dihedral group of the heptagon) is ours, not Taneyev’s. The rules it draws come from §§90 and 128 and are computed by the same code as the charts.
