Ficta, hexachords and mi–fa

The interval numbers on this site count steps, not semitones, so a Jv chart or an imaginary cantus works the same whatever the whole and half steps are. Half steps matter in four places: a 4th or 5th that is really a tritone, an awkward step in one line, the leading tone at a cadence, and whether a canon’s answer copies the half steps exactly. This page explains them in the old terms, hexachords and musica ficta, and in modern keys.

In the notebook: the examples linked below are Cho’s own, on the Notebook page.

Hexachords and mi–fa

A hexachord is six notes, ut re mi fa sol la, with one half step in the middle: always mi–fa. Singers of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries were taught to read melodies through these six syllables, so a half step was “mi–fa” wherever it fell. There were three hexachords [Schulter, section 1.3]:

HexachordNotes, ut to laIts mi–faIts B
Natural, on CC D E F G AE–Fnone
Hard, on GG A B C D EB–CB♮ (mi)
Soft, on FF G A B♭ C DA–B♭B♭ (fa)

Only B differs: it is mi in the hard hexachord and fa in the soft one. Moving from one hexachord to another in mid-line is called mutation. A note outside all three, such as F♯, C♯ or E♭, is musica ficta, “feigned music”: the singer imagines a hexachord placed elsewhere, so that the note becomes its mi or fa. F♯ is mi of a hexachord on D, and E♭ is fa of one on B♭.

Mi contra fa is a mi in one voice against a fa in another, at what should be a perfect interval. B (mi) against F (fa) is a tritone or a diminished 5th, and B♮ against B♭ is an augmented unison. Avoiding “mi contra fa in perfect consonances” [Schulter, section 2.4] is the first of the two reasons for ficta below.

In a modern key, keep the idea and drop the syllables. Every key has one pair of notes a tritone apart: in C, B and F; in G, F♯ and C; in D minor, E and B♭ (and C♯ with G when the leading tone is raised). That pair is the key’s mi and fa. Wherever an interval number says 4th or 5th and both notes of the pair are involved, check it.

A rule of thumb often quoted with the hexachords, of uncertain origin, is Una nota sopra La semper est canendum Fa, “one note above la is always sung fa” [Duffin]. A line in the natural hexachord that goes one step above A and turns back takes B♭, not B♮, so it doesn’t outline F–B.

The two reasons for ficta

Medieval theory gives two reasons for adding a note outside the system: necessity (causa necessitatis) and beauty (causa pulchritudinis). Schulter calls them “the two categories of musica ficta which Ugolino describes” [Schulter, section 2.3], that is, Ugolino of Orvieto, in the fifteenth century; his own text is not checked here. Duffin explains both briefly [Duffin].

Necessity: a perfect interval, or a bad step

Ficta is needed when, without it, a perfect consonance would be diminished or augmented, or a line would make an augmented step. The notebook already does this:

  • Against the other voice. IC p. 6, top, is in D minor, so B♭ is in the key. In bar 3, P has B♮ over the cantus’s E on beats 2 and 4: B♭ would be a diminished 5th, and B♮ makes a perfect 5th.
  • Against the copy. In the same canon, R’s bar 5 copies P’s E as B♭ while the cantus holds B♭. B♮ would be the exact copy, but it would clash with the cantus. Necessity comes before an exact copy.
  • No outlined tritone. In IC p. 29, D dorian, bar 8 of the upper voice runs B♭ C D E F. With B♮ the run would span a tritone, B to F.
  • No augmented 2nd. In IC p. 10, R’s free cadence in bar 9 goes C♯ B♮ C♯, where B♭ to C♯ would be an augmented 2nd. IC p. 15 does the same in G minor with E♮ F♯ G in bars 18–19. This is the ascending melodic minor.

Beauty: a half step into the cadence

At a cadence two voices move from an imperfect consonance to a perfect one. Make the imperfect one as close as it can be, so that one voice moves by a half step: a major 6th opening to an octave, or a minor 3rd closing to a unison [Schulter, section 2.2, “closest approach”]. In a modern key that is the raised leading tone.

  • IC p. 29, D dorian, bars 3–4 and 10–11: C♯ over the cantus’s E, a major 6th, opens to D over D. Inverted at the octave, the same C♯ is a minor 3rd below E and closes to the unison, so one ficta serves both versions.
  • IC p. 28, D minor: the C♯ leading tone is copied into R. A canon at the octave copies every half step exactly, so the copy keeps it.

Beauty is optional and necessity is not. Leaving out a cadential sharp gives an older, modal cadence; a missing necessity sharp or flat is a wrong note.

Canons: exact copy or in-key copy

A canon at the octave copies every half step automatically. At any other interval, R can copy P in two ways:

Exact
Every interval keeps its size, half steps included. At the 5th above, R is then in the dominant key.
In key
Every interval keeps its step number, but R stays in the key signature, so a half step in P can come out as a whole step in R.

Both are legitimate, and the Jv and ICF rules work the same for both, because they count steps. Zarlino seems to distinguish the two as fuga and imitatione [Zarlino 1558, Part III; the chapter is still to be checked].

The hexachord trick at the 5th

The natural hexachord, C to A, moved up a 5th is the hard hexachord, G to E, half step and all. So if P stays inside C D E F G A, a canon at the 5th above is exact without any ficta. Only P’s B breaks it: the exact answer is F♯, the in-key answer F. Cho’s notebook says the same on IC p. 17, “Hexachords / modulations for canon @ 5th”: “no leading tone in the proposta!” The soft hexachord moved up a 5th is the natural one, so P in F with B♭ answers exactly in C. The same page warns: “Mi contra fa creates problems in strict imitation.”

In another key, read the table by scale degree: at the 5th above, P’s leading tone is the problem note; at the 5th below, P’s 4th degree is.

The spiral, and two ways out

An exact canon at the 5th above answers every note a 5th sharper. If R’s new sharp is copied again, as happens when the voices trade places or the canon goes round, the next answer needs one sharp more, and the canon spirals round the circle of 5ths. So every canon at the 5th or 12th has to settle the mi–fa question again and again. There are two ways out:

  1. Never make the problem note. Keep P inside the hexachord that maps cleanly (C to A for an answer a 5th above), so the exact answer needs no ficta at all. This is the strict solution, at the cost of a narrower line.
  2. Copy in key by default. Let R stay in the key signature and make a copy exact only where the in-key note would clash (necessity), or where you want the color (as in the next example). Decide case by case, but on purpose.

Within a passage, keep to one side: an exact F♯ in one bar followed by a B♭ for color two bars later swings R across the key and back. Canon at the 5th above, with ficta on the Notebook page shows the choice.

Most of the notebook’s canons at the 5th and 12th mix the two, and that is fine. What matters is that each in-key copy is a choice: either the exact note would clash, as in IC p. 6 above, or R is meant to stay in the key. The canons at the octave, IC p. 17 (C♯ in R for every F♯ in P) and the unfinished IC p. 14 copy exactly throughout.

A checklist for strict style in modern keys

  1. Know the key’s mi–fa pair, the two notes a tritone apart.
  2. Wherever the interval number says unison, 4th, 5th or octave, check the pair. A diminished or augmented one is wrong unless it is a passing or neighbor dissonance. Fix it with ficta or a different note. (Necessity.)
  3. In each line: no augmented 2nd, no tritone leap, no chromatic half step, and don’t outline the pair. In minor, raise 6 and 7 going up to the tonic, and use them unraised going down. (Necessity.)
  4. At each cadence: major 6th to octave or minor 3rd to unison, so the leading tone is raised. (Beauty.)
  5. In canons at the 5th or 12th: for each copy of the problem note in the table, choose exact or in key. Use exact unless it clashes or pulls R out of the key you want, or keep P inside the hexachord that maps cleanly.
  6. No cross relations: if one voice has F♯, no other voice has F♮ on the beats around it.
  7. In invertible counterpoint, check the ficta again in the derivative. A cadential sharp in one version may be wrong, or not needed, in the other.

Cross relations and the English cadence

The ban on cross relations belongs to the strict style that Fux and Taneyev teach, and it was never universal. A cross relation is what you get when the two reasons for ficta collide: one voice raises the leading tone for a cadence (beauty) while another, falling through the same degree, keeps it low. English composers from Tallis and Byrd to Gibbons often kept both, sometimes in the same chord. The pattern is called the English cadence: in C, B♭ sounding against or next to the B♮ of the dominant. It seems to have been thought old-fashioned by the 1590s; Wikipedia says Morley called it “stale” in 1597, which is not checked against Morley here. In exercises, follow the rule, since it is part of the style the method is built on. In free composition a cross relation is a choice of style, and an English one.
