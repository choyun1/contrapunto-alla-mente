Bathe’s canon table (c. 1596)

William Bathe’s “generall Table comprehending two parts in one, of all kindes vpon all plaine Songs” (A Briefe Introduction to the Skill of Song, London, c. 1596) is a lookup for a two-part canon over a plain song (a cantus firmus) sung beneath both parts, the follower entering one plain-song note after the leader. It is the imaginary-cantus method of the Write a canon page, done for every case at once, over three centuries before Taneyev.

In the notebook: canons over a cantus firmus, where IC p. 5’s canon at the 5th above keeps to Bathe’s cells.

How to read it

Bathe counts from 1, as musicians name intervals: 1 is a unison, 5 a 5th, 8 an octave. Octaves are ignored, so a 12th counts as a 5th.

Place (the columns)
The interval of the canon: where the follower sings, measured from the leader. Place 5 up is a canon at the 5th above. A place down shares the column of its complement, so 4 down sits with 5 up (a 4th below and a 5th above name the same note).
Course (the rows)
How the plain song moves to its next note. Course 1 repeats the note, 2 up is a step up, 3 down is a 3rd down, 8 is an octave. A course down shares the row of its complement, so 2 down sits with 7 up.
The cell
The intervals the leader may sing above the plain song’s current note, so that the follower, copying it a note later, is consonant with the plain song’s next note.

For example, a canon at the 5th above (place 5) over a plain song going C to D (course 2): the cell is 3 5. If the leader sings E (a 3rd above C), the follower answers B over D, a 6th. If it sings G (a 5th), the follower answers D, an octave. A unison would answer G over D, a 4th, and a 6th would answer a 9th, so those are left out.

The table, as Bathe printed it

* Course 5, place 2 is printed 3 6. It is a known misprint; the editors correct it to 1 6, which is also what the rule below gives. The other 55 cells match the rule as printed.

The canon move chords in the table

Bathe’s columns hold the move chords of the Canon proposta page, exactly. Once the leader sings a note, the follower’s note one plain-song note later is fixed, so for that bar the plain song and the follower are a two-voice canon, with the plain song as the proposta below. The plain song’s next note must be in that canon’s move chord, the same four note names the left hand finds. Click any number in the table: the cells in its column holding the same number are that chord.

Read across a cell instead and it is the leader’s side: the consonance chord over the plain song (A C E G over C, the right hand’s shape) with only the notes that also fit the imaginary cantus kept. So every cell is a run of that chord in thirds: 1 3 5 6 is all of it, 1 3 5 or 1 3 6 three notes, 3 5 or 1 6 two, and 5 or 6 just one.

One row of the table, notated

Pick how the plain song moves. Each card is one canon interval: in the first bar the leader (P) sings every note the cell allows over the plain song (CF); in the second the follower (R) copies them, moved by the canon’s interval, over the plain song’s next note. The gray I.C.F. is that next note moved back by the canon’s interval: P against it is R against the CF, a bar early. Under each card, every consonance the leader could try, and what the follower makes of it.

From Bathe’s grid to Taneyev’s rows

The same table in Taneyev’s terms

Taneyev counts from 0 (unison 0, 3rd 2, 5th 4, octave 7) and calls a shift Jv. The follower’s note against the next plain-song note is the leader’s note against that next note moved down by the place: the imaginary cantus. So each bar is a shift of the leader’s interval above the plain song, m, to its interval above the imaginary cantus, n = m + Jv, with

Jv = (place − 1) − (course − 1) = the canon’s interval − the plain song’s move

which is the guide interval of the Write a canon page. Within the octave it is one of seven numbers, so the 56 cells are only seven rows, one per shift. Both parts stay above the plain song, so Jv is a direct shift, 0 to +6 (or the same plus an octave).
