Canon handshapes

In a two-voice canon, the proposta’s next note has to be consonant with the note the risposta is singing now. Up to octaves, that leaves exactly four note names for every interval of entrance, and five at the unison. They fit under one hand, and they stack in thirds as one chord. Intervals are zero-indexed (step 1, 3rd 2, octave 7).

Worked examples in the notebook: two-part canons and infinite canons.

The rule

  • Thumb on the risposta. The risposta is singing your note from one delay ago, moved by the interval of entrance. Put your thumb on that note.
  • Above it, right hand; below it, left hand. Make a C–E–G–A shape from the thumb: thumb on the unison or octave, index on the 3rd, middle on the 5th, ring on the 6th. The same keys an octave further out on your side work too.
  • At the unison, use both hands, with the thumbs on the same key. That covers every note name except the two a step away.

Fingers

  • 1 3Thumb and middle finger are perfect: don’t use the same one twice in a row while both voices move
  • 2 4Index and ring finger are imperfect: use them freely
  • R is the risposta’s note, P the proposta’s note one delay ago; the lit keys are the notes the proposta may move to

Canon

The three shapes

With the risposta on middle C. The chord on the right holds the same note names stacked in thirds, which is easier to read at a glance. Its notes are names only: play each one on your own side of the risposta.

Why it is always four

If the risposta enters at Jv and the proposta moves by a span across the delay, the voices then sound n = Jv − span (see Intervals). The next note is consonant when n is a consonance, and up to octaves there are four: unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th. So the allowed spans are those four turned upside down (the minus sign) and moved by Jv. Neither step changes how many there are.

The minus sign is why the left hand mirrors the right. Moving by Jv is the thumb going from your old note to the risposta’s.

Lay the seven note names out by thirds, C E G B D F A, and close the circle. The consonances seen from one note (unison, 3rd, 5th, and the 6th, which is a 3rd below) are four neighbors in a row. Turning and moving keeps neighbors together, so the allowed names always stack in thirds as a seventh chord. From the risposta on C, that is Am7 above and Fmaj7 below.

At the unison nothing fixes which voice is on top, so both sides count. They share the unison, 3rd and 6th, because inversion at the octave swaps the 3rd and the 6th. They differ only in the 5th, which inverts to a 4th, a dissonance. So the union is five neighbors on the circle, F A C E G: a ninth chord with the risposta’s note in the middle.

Canons that share note names

Measured from your own old note, there are only seven sets of four names, so canons pair up. A canon at an interval above uses the same names as the canon at the interval below that stacks with it to a 10th (or a 3rd). This is inversion at the 10th again. The register still differs: in one canon the notes lie below the risposta, in the other above.
