Introduction

What this site is for, where the practice comes from, and where to read more. Every source named here is listed, with a link where there is one, at the end of the page.

The problem

To improvise counterpoint is to choose, in time and without writing, a note that is right against the other voices. Over a slow cantus firmus with one voice added, a trained singer can do this by ear. Two things make it much harder.

Canon. In a canon the note you sing now is sung again by the follower a few beats later, against music that has not happened yet. Every note has to be good twice: against what is sounding now, and against what will be sounding when it comes back. Over a cantus firmus, the second check is against a CF note you have not reached.

Invertible counterpoint. If two voices are to be swapped, or one moved by a 10th or a 12th, every interval between them has to be good in two places at once. A 6th that is fine as written becomes a 7th at the 12th; a 5th becomes a 4th at the octave.

Working this out note by note is slow, and too slow to do while singing or playing. Both the historical singers and Taneyev answered it in the same way: they reduced each case to a small table of intervals that are safe, and learned the table until it no longer had to be looked up. This site is a trainer for that. It shows the tables, checks what you write against them, and drills them at the keyboard. It does not write counterpoint for you.

Singing upon the book

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, much polyphony was never written down. Singers in church choirs added counterpoint to a chant read from the choirbook, a practice called cantare super librum, singing upon the book. Italian writers called improvised counterpoint contrapunto alla mente, counterpoint in the mind, as against counterpoint on paper. The site takes its name from that phrase.

Johannes Tinctoris, in his Liber de arte contrapuncti (1477), sets singing upon the book beside written composition, the res facta. Margaret Bent’s reading of the passage is the useful one here: in written music every voice must agree with every other, but when several singers improvise upon the book, each one need only agree with the tenor [Bent 1983]. That is the rule the imaginary-cantus method keeps. Each added voice is checked against one line, so the check can be done in the head.

The fullest surviving account of how this was taught is a mid-sixteenth-century manuscript treatise by the Portuguese musician Vicente Lusitano, studied and edited by Philippe Canguilhem [Canguilhem 2011, 2013]. Lusitano also printed a short introduction to counterpoint, the Introduttione facilissima (Rome, 1553). Canguilhem argues that the lines singers were expected to improvise were more elaborate than had been assumed, and that the line between improvising and composing has to be redrawn. Rob Wegman traces how, in the Low Countries after 1450, the written composition and its named composer came to be valued over the improvising singer [Wegman 1996].

Two parts in one upon a plainsong

English teachers of the same period wrote down the canon case. William Bathe’s A Briefe Introduction to the Skill of Song (London, c. 1596) prints a “generall Table comprehending two parts in one, of all kindes vpon all plaine Songs”: for each interval of canon and each way the plainsong can move, the intervals the leader may sing. A singer who knows the table can make a two-voice canon over any plainsong. The Bathe’s table page notates it and shows that its 56 cells are seven Taneyev shifts [Bathe; Rotem and Schab].

The next year, Thomas Morley’s A Plaine and Easie Introduction to Practicall Musicke (London, 1597) gave its whole second part to descant, “and to sing two parts in one upon a plainsong or ground” [Morley 1597].

From the voice to the page

Over the next two centuries the teaching of counterpoint moved onto paper. Johann Joseph Fux’s Gradus ad Parnassum (Vienna, 1725) set out the five species as written exercises, and it shaped how counterpoint was taught from then on. The keyboard kept an improvised tradition for longer: in the Naples conservatories, students learned to realize a figured or unfigured bass at sight from partimenti, written basses meant to be played into full textures. Robert Gjerdingen and Giorgio Sanguinetti brought that tradition back to scholarly attention [Gjerdingen 2007; Sanguinetti 2012]. A partimento section is planned for this site.

Taneyev

Sergei Taneyev taught counterpoint at the Moscow Conservatory, and his treatise on invertible counterpoint appeared in Moscow in 1909. Its Russian title means “movable counterpoint of the strict style”; the English translation of 1962 calls it Convertible Counterpoint in the Strict Style [Taneyev 1909/1962]. His second book, on canon, was put together after his death and published in 1929 [Taneyev 1929].

Taneyev’s step was to make every case one calculation. He counts intervals from 0 (unison 0, 3rd 2, 5th 4, octave 7), so that moving one voice by a shift Jv turns an interval m into n = m + Jv. Each shift then has one chart: which intervals stay consonant, which become dissonant, and how a suspension has to be written so that both versions are correct. Bathe’s table is seven of those charts. A canon over a cantus firmus becomes a series of shifts, one per CF note, through what he calls the imaginary cantus.

For an improviser this matters because there is less to memorize. Instead of a separate table for every canon and every CF motion, there are a handful of charts and one subtraction to find which chart applies. Christopher Segall’s article is the clearest short introduction in English [Segall 2014], and Jacob Gran’s video course Movable Counterpoint works through the method with examples [Gran 2024]. The site’s rules follow both and cite Taneyev by section.

The practice today

Improvised Renaissance counterpoint is taught again, at early-music schools and in university theory courses. Peter Schubert’s chapter on Renaissance counterpoint pedagogy is a good overview of what the treatises asked of students [Schubert 2002]. Two collections gather current work: Improvising Early Music, by Rob Wegman, Johannes Menke and Peter Schubert (2014), and Studies in Historical Improvisation: From Cantare super Librum to Partimenti, edited by Massimiliano Guido (2017). Frauke Jürgensen describes teaching it in a modern university course [Jürgensen 2019].

How to use the site

  1. Learn the shifts on the Jv charts, starting with the common ones (0, −7, −9, −11).
  2. See which melodic moves a canon allows on Canon proposta, and find them under the hand on Handshapes.
  3. Write a canon over a cantus firmus, bar by bar, on Canon over a CF. Bathe’s table is the same method for every case at once.
  4. Practice at the keyboard with the Drills, until the allowed notes come without counting.
  5. Read worked examples, with the imaginary cantus in gray, in the Notebook.
