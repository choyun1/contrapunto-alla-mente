Introduction

What this site is for, where the practice comes from, and where to read more. Sources are cited by a short name in brackets, such as [Bent 1983], and listed in full at the end.

The problem

To improvise counterpoint is to choose, in time and without writing, a note that is right against the other voices. Over a slow cantus firmus with one voice added, a trained singer can do this by ear. Two things make it much harder.

Canon. In a canon the note you sing now is sung again by the follower a few beats later, against music that has not happened yet. Every note has to be good twice: against what is sounding now, and against what will be sounding when it comes back. Over a cantus firmus, the second check is against a CF note you have not reached.

Invertible counterpoint. If two voices are to be swapped, or one moved by a 10th or a 12th, every interval between them has to be good in two places at once. A 6th that is fine as written becomes a 7th at the 12th; a 5th becomes a 4th at the octave.

Working this out note by note is slow, too slow to do while singing or playing. William Bathe in the sixteenth century and Sergei Taneyev in the twentieth answered it the same way: they reduced each case to a small table of safe intervals, to be learned until it no longer had to be looked up. This site provides tools to practice that. It shows the tables, checks what you write against them, and drills them at the keyboard. It does not write counterpoint for you.

Singing upon the book

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, much polyphony was never written down. Singers added counterpoint to a chant read from the choirbook, a practice called cantare super librum, singing upon the book. In Italian treatises of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, the usual term for unwritten counterpoint was contrapunto alla mente, counterpoint in the mind [Morucci 2013, p. 2]. Adriano Banchieri describes it in 1614, in the papal chapel in Rome, at Loreto and in many other chapels. In Morucci’s translation, when the singers sing counterpoint alla mente above a bass, “no one knows what his fellow singer shall sing, but everybody, following certain previously shared indications, produces a very pleasing sound” [Banchieri 1614, p. 230, quoted in Morucci 2013, pp. 5–6 and n. 14].

Johannes Tinctoris set out the difference between written and improvised counterpoint in 1477 [Tinctoris 1477, Book II, ch. 20]. In a written piece, the res facta, every voice has to be consonant with every other voice. When several singers improvise upon the book, each one only has to be consonant with the tenor, the voice that carries the chant. Margaret Bent’s article explains the two terms [Bent 1983]. The methods on this site use the same idea: every check is between two lines only, which is a check a singer can make in the head.

The fullest surviving account of how improvised counterpoint was taught is a manuscript treatise from the mid-sixteenth century by the Portuguese musician Vicente Lusitano, studied and edited by Philippe Canguilhem [Canguilhem 2011; Canguilhem 2013]. Lusitano also printed a short introduction to counterpoint, the Introduttione facilissima (Rome, 1553) [Lusitano 1553]. Canguilhem argues that the lines singers were expected to improvise were more complex than scholars had assumed, and that this calls for a new look at how improvisation relates to composition. Rob Wegman traces how ideas of the composer and of composition changed in the Low Countries between 1450 and 1500, as written works came to be set apart from improvised ones [Wegman 1996].

Two parts in one upon a plainsong

English teachers of the same period wrote down rules for the canon case. William Bathe’s A Briefe Introduction to the Skill of Song (London, c. 1596) includes “A generall Table comprehending two parts in one, of all kindes vpon all plaine Songs” [Bathe c. 1596, heading of the table]. For each interval of canon and each interval the plainsong moves by, it lists the intervals the leader may sing. The same heading says that with the table “the vpper part is made, and neuer booked”: made up, that is, without being written down. The Bathe’s table page notates the table and shows that its 56 cells reduce to seven Taneyev shifts [Rotem and Schab 2024].

A year later, Thomas Morley published A Plaine and Easie Introduction to Practicall Musicke (London, 1597), in three parts. Its title page says that the second part “treateth of descante and to sing two parts in one vpon a plainsong or ground” [Morley 1597, title page].

Other traditions

Singing upon the book is one tradition among several. Written exercises are another: Johann Joseph Fux’s Gradus ad Parnassum (Vienna, 1725) teaches counterpoint through five species of written exercise, and it long remained a standard textbook [Fux 1725]. Written study did not simply replace improvising, though: Banchieri in 1614 and Lodovico Zacconi in 1622 still describe singers improvising over a bass [Morucci 2013, pp. 5–6].

Keyboard players had their own tradition of improvised counterpoint. In the conservatories of eighteenth-century Naples, students learned from partimenti, bass lines written to be realized at the keyboard into full textures. Robert Gjerdingen and Giorgio Sanguinetti brought these exercises back to scholarly attention [Gjerdingen 2007; Sanguinetti 2012]. What this site plans to add next is listed on the home page.

Taneyev

Sergei Taneyev taught counterpoint at the Moscow Conservatory. His treatise on invertible counterpoint was published in Moscow in 1909. Its Russian title, Podvizhnoi kontrapunkt strogogo pis’ma, means literally movable counterpoint of the strict style; the 1962 English translation calls it Convertible Counterpoint in the Strict Style [Taneyev 1909/1962]. His book on canon was compiled after his death by Viktor Belyaev and published in 1929 [Taneyev 1929].

Taneyev’s contribution was a complete and general account of the interval choices: one method that covers every case. He counts intervals from 0 (unison 0, 3rd 2, 5th 4, octave 7), so that moving one voice by a shift Jv turns an interval m into n = m + Jv. Each shift then has one chart: which intervals stay consonant, which become dissonant, and how a suspension has to be written so that both versions are correct. Bathe’s table is seven of those charts. A canon over a cantus firmus becomes a series of shifts, one per CF note, through what Taneyev calls the imaginary cantus.

For an improviser, the result is much less to memorize, and no arbitrary trial and error. Instead of a separate table for every canon and every CF motion, there are a handful of charts, and one subtraction tells you which chart applies. Christopher Segall’s article is the clearest short introduction in English [Segall 2014], and Jacob Gran’s video course Movable Counterpoint works through the method with examples [Gran 2024]. The site’s rules follow both and cite Taneyev by section.

The practice today

Improvised Renaissance counterpoint is taught again, at early-music schools and in university theory courses. Peter Schubert’s chapter on Renaissance counterpoint pedagogy is a good overview of what the treatises asked of students [Schubert 2002]. Two collections gather recent work: Improvising Early Music, by Rob Wegman, Johannes Menke and Peter Schubert [Wegman, Menke and Schubert 2014], and Studies in Historical Improvisation: From Cantare super Librum to Partimenti, edited by Massimiliano Guido [Guido 2017]. Frauke Jürgensen describes teaching it in a modern university course [Jürgensen 2019].

How to use the site

  1. Learn the shifts on the Jv charts, starting with the common ones (0, −7, −9, −11).
  2. See which melodic moves a canon allows on Canon proposta, and find them under the hand on Handshapes.
  3. Write a canon over a cantus firmus, bar by bar, on Canon over a CF. Bathe’s table is the same method for every case at once.
  4. Practice at the keyboard with the Drills, until the allowed notes come without counting.
  5. Read worked examples, with the imaginary cantus in gray, in the Notebook.
